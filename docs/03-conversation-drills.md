# Conversation Drills

This chapter is the heart of the guide. Drills, each with a prompt to paste
and a way to run it, plus the correction loop that turns chatting into
learning. Fifteen minutes, one drill at a time (chapter 1), same drill all
week — boredom is a feature.

## The correction loop (why this works)

Raw conversation with AI is pleasant and nearly worthless, because you can
be vague and the model will be polite and guess. The fix is the loop:

1. **You attempt** — a sentence, in the target language, imperfect on
   purpose.
2. **The partner replies in kind** — in the language, same level.
3. **Only at the end, the corrections table** — what you said | a more
   natural way | why. Not interrupting every error. Interrogating your
   conversation kills flow; a clean table preserves it *and* shows
   patterns.
4. **You harvest** — one line from the table goes into your phrase or
   vocab file (chapter 4). One. Not all five. One deliberate pick beats
   five scattershot saves.

Beginner tip: run the loop **without** the table for two minutes of free
flow first, then with it. Flow first, feedback second.

## How to make the drills harder

For any drill, there's a dial:

- **Level 1, scripted**: partner asks predictable questions ("Do you have
  any pets?"), you answer from memory.
- **Level 2, constrained**: you may not use certain words (no "very", no
  past tense — as appropriate), forcing new constructions.
- **Level 3, surprise**: partner answers at normal speed and occasionally
  adds an unexpected follow-up question. Recovering from surprise is
  rocket fuel for fluency.

Wave between 1 and 3 within weeks. Every session includes a little
discomfort, never all discomfort.

## The drills

### Drill 1 — The Daily Round

The warmest, easiest drill, built on your ordinary day:

> Give me a day in [my work] in [LANGUAGE]. Ask me five questions about my
> usual routine — when I wake up, what I eat, how I get to work. Ask one at
> a time, in [LANGUAGE], wait for my answer, then the next. After the five,
> tell me in two lines which vocab I'm missing most.

Why it works first: the content is *your life*, which needs no invention.
You're not thinking up something to say about penguins in the Arctic; you
already know the answer, in your own language.

### Drill 2 — The Shopkeeper

Practical speech: price, quantity, polite refusal.

> You are a market vendor in [CITY IN TARGET-LANGUAGE COUNTRY]. I am a
> customer with no basket and $15. Improvise; do not break character;
> everything in [LANGUAGE]. Occasionally ask about allergies or receipts
> when it fits.

Runs on two minutes with a built-in role — you can do this on a bad day.
Add a second customer mid-week so the vocabulary has to stretch.

### Drill 3 — The Job Interview / Clinic Check-in

Useful for both travel and daily life:

> Play a receptionist at a clinic/hotel/apartment rental. I am [patient/
> guest/tenant]. Walk me through a normal intake process entirely in
> [LANGUAGE]: date of birth, reason for coming, how I pay, what happens
> next. When I'm stuck, hold eye contact (so to speak) and let me try
> twice before offering one hint word.

Hint after two tries: that's the entire teaching philosophy of this guide
in one invented rule.

### Drill 4 — Twenty Questions (reversed)

You are the answer. This forces you to *ask* questions — the most neglected
skill in beginners and the most valuable in the world.

> We play twenty questions, but I am the one who doesn't know the secret
> object and you know it — which means I ask in [LANGUAGE] and you only
> answer "yes", "no", or "sometimes, it depends". Count my questions. If I
> run out, reveal slowly, in [LANGUAGE], with fanfare.

Asking forces word order under pressure, which no word list teaches.

### Drill 5 — The Story Wreck

One sentence at a time, together, in the target language: partner writes
three pages of a silly story, you write the next page with one sentence,
partner writes the next, and so on. Nothing sacred, everything absurd —
absurd is memorable. Five sentences of story = a surprising amount of
grammar negotiation with zero pressure.

### Drill 6 — The Debates With Myself

For the plateau weeks (chapter 8) when you feel too advanced-easy for the
drills above but too awkward for real life:

> Pick a low-stakes controversy — dogs vs cats, early vs late riser, window
> vs aisle. Argue a 50/50 split for one minute each side, entirely in
> [LANGUAGE]. Then grill me on which side I chose and make me defend it
> with two reasons and one concession.

Concessions ("yes, but you could also say...") push conditional grammar
exactly where A2/B1 learners stall.

## The two rules that make all of them work

1. **Same drill, all week.** Repetition turns effort into comfort; the
   vocabulary of the drill gets a chance to settle.
2. **Speak or type, then say it aloud.** Even silently mouthing the words
   after you type them engages real pronunciation muscles.
