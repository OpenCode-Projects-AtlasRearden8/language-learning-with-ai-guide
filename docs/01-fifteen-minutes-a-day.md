# Fifteen Minutes a Day

The single biggest reason people don't learn a language isn't difficulty —
it's the size of the plan. A ninety-minute study block requires a free
evening, a quiet house, and motivation to spare. Fifteen minutes requires a
clock and a pulse.

## Why fifteen minutes works

- It fits in real life: one coffee break, one bus ride, one stretch between
  tasks. On a twelve-hour nursing shift you still get fifteen minutes.
- It happens daily, and **daily beats long**. Your brain consolidates
  language between sessions; twelve sessions of 15 minutes outperform one
  session of 180 for remembering and speaking.
- It is too small to dread. The plan doesn't fail because of you — it fails
  because it was designed for a person who doesn't exist.

## The starter plan (weeks 1–4)

Fifteen minutes, four parts. Set a timer; part boundaries are soft.

| Minutes | Part | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | **Warm-up** | Open your vocab file (`scripts/vocab.py`) and review whatever is due. Small, fast, pretend it's a warm-up tap on the shoulder. |
| 2–9 | **Core drill** | One conversation drill from chapter 3 — *the same drill all week.* Repetition, not novelty, builds the muscle. |
| 9–13 | **Input** | Something you consume: chapter 6 media trick, or read your own phrase list aloud. |
| 13–15 | **Close** | Add 2–3 words or one phrase you met today to your vocab or phrase file (chapter 4). Close with a sentence of *your own* — one sentence, in the language, about your actual day. |

The last two minutes matter more than they look. That single sentence is
the difference between studying a language and *starting to have one*.

## The weekly rhythm

Not every day is the same, and that's fine. A sustainable week:

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Mon–Fri | The starter plan above |
| Saturday | **Long input day** — a film scene, a song with lyrics, a market menu (chapter 6). Stretches to 30–45 minutes. |
| Sunday | **Ten-minute maintenance only.** Vocab review, one sentence about your week. Or skip guilt-free if you did the rest — see chapter 8. |

## The weekly plan cards

Each week gets one card. A card is small on purpose — one week, one focus,
one set of tiny checkboxes. Write it at the start of the week, tape it where
you'll see it (fridge, notebook, checklist app), and check the boxes as you
go. Checking a box is easy enough to survive a bad week.

Three ready-made cards live in `templates/weekly-plan-cards.md`:

1. **Card 1 — Word Week**: the starter habit itself. Every day is a short
   drill plus two new words. Lightest possible lift.
2. **Card 2 — Sentence Week**: every answer must be a full sentence, and you
   start a one-line diary in the language.
3. **Card 3 — Listening Week**: the ear, not the mouth — AI speaks first,
   you follow, and the weekend is one long input session.

Use Card 1 your first week. After that, pick the card that matches what you
most need — week 5 and beyond, write your own by copying the format and
swapping the focus. Writing a new card takes about three minutes and is a
mini-lesson in planning your own practice.

## When fifteen minutes is genuinely impossible

Bad shifts happen. The minimum viable session is **one thing**: three
vocabulary reviews *or* one spoken sentence *or* two minutes of a song in
the language. That's it. A minimum session keeps the streak of identity —
"someone who speaks a little Spanish" — alive, and identity is what brings
you back tomorrow. Chapter 8 has the full philosophy; the short version is
that the streak is the goal today, volume can wait.

## Upgrade path (weeks 5+)

Once the habit holds, grow one variable at a time — never two:

- **Add speech**: say everything out loud instead of typing it (chapter 2
  covers voice options late; typing + speaking-aloud is fine early).
- **Add difficulty**: switch drills from scripted to surprise (chapter 3).
- **Add length**: 15 → 20 minutes. Stop before it feels heavy.

Never sacrifice the daily-ness for the ambition. Every time the plan gets
heavier than your life, the plan loses.
