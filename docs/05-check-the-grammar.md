# Check the Grammar

AI models generate fluent, confident, occasionally fictional grammar rules.
This chapter is a *filter*: how to use AI's speed to find an answer, and
real sources to confirm it — before it becomes the fossil in your muscle
memory.

If the sister guide's chapter 4 (*Catching Hallucinations*) taught you how
to catch an AI inventing facts, this chapter is the language-learning
application of the same skill. Read that first if this all feels abstract.

## The rule of the chapter

> **Never keep a grammar rule unless you've seen it twice outside the AI.**

Twice. In real sources. Not in the same chat, not in the same week. The
rule of two keeps you from fossilizing an elegant-sounding myth.

## What "real sources" means in plain terms

You don't need academic linguistics. Good independent checks, easy to
find and for free:

- **A reputable dictionary** with grammar notes — for Spanish the RAE/
  WordReference entries; for French the Larousse entries; for Japanese
  Jisho.org. Dictionary example sentences are written by people who
  actually know.
- **A grammar reference site or handbook** (many public libraries offer
  free access to grammar e-tools with a library card — check your local
  branch).
- **Native text, unedited**: news articles, song lyrics performed by
  native artists, government pages, school menus. If a structure appears
  (or never appears) across ten clearly native texts, that's evidence.
- **Textbook sample sentences** you can photograph in a library or used
  bookstore — even a different textbook than yours.

The pattern behind every check: **independent, written-for-humans, not
generated for you.**

## AI's job vs. real sources' job

| AI (first draft, fast) | Real sources (final word, slow) |
|---|---|
| Explains a rule plausibly | Confirms the rule exists |
| Generates 20 practice examples | Provides 20 real examples |
| Catches your typos fast | Arbitrates when two explanations disagree |
| Answers "is this odd?" | Publishes the exceptions on record |

Use AI like a chatty, encouraging tutor who *sometimes* misremembers
citations; use real sources like the calm reference desk agent upstairs.

## The check routine (two minutes, saved mistakes)

Run it whenever an AI gives you a *rule* — not for every correction of
your own sentences, that gets exhausting. The triggers are:

1. It states a rule ("In this language, the adjective goes after the noun
   **always**").
2. It invents an exception ("except these eleven words, which are
   irregular") — exceptions on demand are where fabrication lives.
3. Two different chats gave you two different rules.
4. You're about to memorize it.

Then:

**Step 1 — ask the AI for its check, but skeptically.**
> What would a reliable printed grammar book (name one) say about this?
> Where do I verify it myself? Give me an example sentence I can search
> for verbatim.

**Step 2 — do the search it suggested, somewhere it doesn't control.**
Search the exact example sentence in quotes in a news archive or search
engine. Zero real results means either the sentence is unnatural *or* the
rule is shady. Both are flags.

**Step 3 — the dictionary game.**
Look up one word involved in the rule at a real dictionary. Read the
example sentences at the bottom of the entry. Two minutes, and the entry
was written by lexicographers, not by probability.

**Step 4 — log the ruling.**
Keep a plain file `grammar-rules-verified.md`. One line per rule:
`rule — where you saw it twice — the day you checked`. It becomes your
personal micro-grammar book inside the same plain-file school.

## An honest example of an AI mistake

You type: "Is it okay to say *'I have twenty years'* for my age?"

The AI says — depending on the run — anything from "not idiomatic, say
it differently" to inventing a rule about when numbers with ages take a
preposition. Some of those inventions are elegant and wrong (or wrong for
your particular language entirely). A real dictionary note about the age
construction, and a couple of real sentences from a newspaper obituary
page, settle it in ninety seconds with sources you can re-open.

The AI is usually *mostly* right, and this process exists to catch the
*mostly*.

## When sources disagree

Sometimes the AI, your textbook, and the reference site really do
disagree — language is spoken by humans before it's settled by
authorities. When that happens:

1. Note the disagreement in `grammar-rules-verified.md`.
2. Find real native text and count. If speakers you'd copy do A ninety
   times and B twice (both in jokes and song lyrics), learn A now, B
   later as a curiosity.
3. Move on. Perfect certainty about why is the enemy of fluency gains —
   converging on *use* matters more.

## The boundary you don't cross

Use this chapter for **grammar rules and facts** (word order, gender,
conjugations, when a tense is correct). Don't demand it for taste. If a
native speaker would say both A and B with a shrug, an AI's "Statement B
is more natural" is fine to accept mid-session. Save your checking energy
for the places where speech can go wrong, not where it can merely go
*slightly different flavors of the same right*.
