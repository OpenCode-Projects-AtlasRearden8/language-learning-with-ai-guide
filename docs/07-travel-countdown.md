# The Travel Countdown

A trip is the best deadline you will ever be given. Ten days before the
plane leaves, you do not need fluency — you need **a short list of real
situations you can survive with grace.** This chapter is that intensive:
focused, driven by your actual itinerary, and finished. No guilt after,
because it was always a sprint.

## The math of ten days

Ten days at fifteen minutes is two and a half hours. That's enough for
roughly 40–60 high-frequency phrases spoken confidently, which is more
than most tourists ever use. The intensive works by being **narrow**:
you will be terrible at discussing philosophy and *putsch*-level fine at
ordering, asking directions, apologizing, and buying medicine.

## Day 1 — the itinerary inventory

Before any study, list your trip's real situations. Take fifteen minutes
with the AI partner if you only know the city and dates:

> I am traveling to [CITY, COUNTRY] for [N] days. My days include: [the
> actual plan — airport arrival, hotel, two work dinners, one long walk,
> a pharmacy visit because I blister easily]. List the 12 most common
> situations a traveler like me will actually face, most frequent first.

Keep *their* list but push back on it. You know your trip. Delete
situations you won't face, add ones you will ("ask whether the soup
contains the one food I'm allergic to" is worth three situations).

## Days 2–8 — one situation per day

Each day: **one situation**, built as a phrase list from the template
(chapter 4's file format). The daily routine hard-charges the starter
plan (chapter 1): two minutes of old review, nine minutes on *today's*
list, four minutes reading it aloud, closing with the one sentence.

For each situation, draft with the partner:

> Here is my situation: [e.g., hotel check-in]. Give me 8 phrases I'd
> genuinely say or hear, ordered by how often they come up. For each: a
> natural version in [LANGUAGE], a straightforward pronunciation line
> (plain syllables, not phonetic symbols), and what I should listen for
> in the reply. Flag any phrase where body language does half the work.

Then run **Drill 2 (Shopkeeper) / Drill 3 (intake)** from chapter 3 in
that exact setting, so phrases attach to a moment, not a list.

After the dialog: `Out of these, which single phrase, if I got it wrong,
would make the whole exchange stall?` Practice that one twice as often.

## Day 9 — the dress rehearsal

Today is not new material. Today is the *whole trip* in one session:

> Run me through my trip, back to back. Situation 1, then a reply, then
> immediately situation 2 — no pausing for me to translate, keep the
> pace roughly like a real day. When I freeze, wait three seconds, then
> ask the question again *more simply*. At the very end, give me my
> "three most likely stalls" — and for each, a rescue phrase ("sorry,
> slower please", "I have limited [LANGUAGE], do you speak English?") I
> should have ready in front, not buried.

Write the rescue phrases on a card you keep in your pocket. This is the
most-used artifact of the entire ten days, more than any list.

## Day 10 — pack it, don't push it

Ten minutes, total: review due vocab, read your phrase lists once aloud,
close the laptop for the trip. Whatever you catch yourself wanting to
add tonight — don't. Crammed words before a flight are sand; rested
phrases are cement. Sleep is part of language learning and always has
been.

## On the trip itself (brief, then back to the method)

- Use the rescue phrases *early*. Announcing "I have limited [LANGUAGE]"
  before you struggle gets you patience you'd otherwise never earn.
- One real conversation on the trip — a taxi driver, a hotel clerk —
  will count more than all ten days. It's the whole method at full
  speed, and imperfection is the entry fee, not a failure.
- Photograph a menu anything that delighted you and bring it home; it's
  your first reading-back session in chapter 6.

## After the trip — the deflation problem

Landing home is a strange crash. The trip is done, and so, it seems, is
the urgency. Two options, either is legitimate (chapter 8 has the full
philosophy):

1. **Feed the fire, one week.** Keep the fifteen minutes, switch from
   travel situations back to the weekly cards from chapter 1. Momentum
   is real and this is the moment it's easiest to keep.
2. **Bank it.** The phrase lists and vocab stay in the plain files. You
   stop for now. The next trip — or the next neighbor who speaks the
   language — restarts you in one session, because nothing was lost:
   it's all still sitting there, like a shelf you built, waiting.

Both are wins. Whichever you choose, the intensive did what it promised:
you went, you spoke, you came back changed a little.
